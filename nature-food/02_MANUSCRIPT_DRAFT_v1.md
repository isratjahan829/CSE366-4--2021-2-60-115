## Abstract (≤150 words; currently ~150)

Information campaigns are central to nutrition and food-safety policy in low- and middle-income countries. However, it is unclear whether what households know translates into what they eat. We surveyed 5,000 households across all eight divisions of Bangladesh (rural, urban and slum strata; October–November 2020). We assessed the knowledge of 5,000 food purchasers, 5,000 cooks, 2,473 adolescents and 581 caregivers, together with 24-hour diets, measured anthropometry and observed hygiene. Knowledge was steeply socially patterned. Compared with having no schooling, higher-secondary education raised the odds of high nutrition knowledge 41-fold among purchasers. Yet knowledge was only weakly associated with dietary diversity `[NEW: AME per 10 pp]`. It was not associated with lower consumption of discretionary foods, which 78–86% of adolescents had eaten in the previous day. The knowledge–diet link was `[NEW: attenuated/absent]` in food-insecure households. Large food-safety gaps persisted: 58% of cooks did not know safe reheating limits. Information-only strategies are unlikely to improve diets unless they are paired with measures that make healthy, safe food affordable.

## Main text

### Introduction (~600 words)

Diets are now the leading risk factor for disease worldwide. Countries undergoing rapid nutrition transition face undernutrition, overweight and diet-related non-communicable diseases at the same time¹,². Bangladesh illustrates this double burden. Over three decades the country has made large gains in food availability and access³. Even so, diets remain dominated by rice, micronutrient-rich foods are under-consumed, and inadequate feeding and hygiene practices continue to undermine child growth⁴,⁵. Unsafe food adds to this burden. Foodborne disease causes a disproportionate share of illness in South Asia⁶, and household-level contamination of complementary foods has been linked to diarrhoea and poor growth in Bangladeshi children⁷,⁸.

Nutrition and food-safety policy in Bangladesh, as in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), relies heavily on behaviour change communication (BCC). BCC rests on an implicit theory of change: closing knowledge gaps will improve food choices, preparation and hygiene. Trials show that intensive BCC can produce lasting gains in infant feeding knowledge⁹. However, evidence from high-income settings suggests that nutrition knowledge is only modestly associated with dietary intake¹⁰,¹¹. Food prices, income and the wider food environment may constrain what knowledgeable consumers can actually buy and eat¹²,¹³. Whether this holds in an LMIC population where large parts of the population are food-insecure, and where healthy diets are unaffordable for many¹⁴, has not been tested at national scale. There is also no nationally representative assessment of consumers' nutrition, cooking and food-safety knowledge in Bangladesh¹⁵. As a result, the targeting of national BCC programmes rests on small, local studies.

Here we use a nationally representative survey of 5,000 households in all eight divisions of Bangladesh to address three questions:
1. How large are gaps in consumers' knowledge of basic nutrition, cooking and food safety, and how are they distributed socially and geographically?
2. Is knowledge associated with dietary diversity, discretionary food consumption and observed hygiene practice?
3. Does household food insecurity weaken any knowledge–diet link?

The survey interviewed the four people who most directly shape household diets: the person who buys food, the person who cooks, adolescents and caregivers of young children. It combined knowledge items based on Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) guidelines¹⁶ with 24-hour dietary recall, measured anthropometry, the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS)¹⁷ and enumerator-observed handwashing and kitchen conditions. Fieldwork took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, so we could also document self-reported changes in hygiene behaviour attributed to public health messaging.

We find that knowledge is strongly patterned by education and wealth. However, it is only weakly associated with what people eat, and `[NEW]` this association largely disappears among food-insecure households. Our results suggest that information alone will not close Bangladesh's diet-quality gap.

### Results (~1,800 words)

#### A nationally representative sample of household food decision-makers
We interviewed members of 5,000 households: 3,989 from 200 rural and urban clusters drawn from the MICS 2019 sampling frame, and 1,011 from 50 slums selected with probability proportional to size (Fig. 1a; Methods). Within households we interviewed:
- 5,000 food purchasers (62.0% male)
- 5,000 primary cooks (99.3% female)
- 2,473 adolescents aged 10–19 (56.6% female)
- 581 primary caregivers of children aged 6–23 months

#### Gaps in specific, actionable nutrition knowledge
Most respondents gave at least partially correct answers to general questions on balanced diets and healthy eating. Knowledge of specific nutrients was poor (Fig. 2):
- Around 62–63% gave incorrect answers on which foods are rich in vitamin A or iron.
- 67% gave incorrect answers about trans-fatty acids and their health effects.

Adolescents in slums had the lowest knowledge: 52.0% were in the lowest tertile. This matters because adolescents also reported the highest consumption of discretionary foods (see below).

#### Knowledge is steeply patterned by education, wealth and place
Education was the strongest correlate of knowledge in every respondent group (Fig. 3a).
- **Purchasers.** Among rural and urban purchasers, the odds of high (vs low) nutrition knowledge compared with no schooling were:
  - 3.9 for primary education
  - 9.7 for secondary education
  - 41.4 for higher-secondary or above
- **Cooks and adolescents.** The corresponding odds ratios for higher-secondary education were 23.2 for cooks and 28.6 for adolescents.

- **Sex.** Female adolescents were more likely than males to have high knowledge (OR 2.4).
- **Geography.** Large differences remained after adjustment:

#### Diets are low in diversity and high in discretionary foods

- Pulses: 53.4%
- Dark green leafy vegetables: 45.2%
- Eggs: 30.3%
- Vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables: 19.5%
- Other fruits: 17.2%
- Dairy: 14.7%

Dietary diversity was inadequate (0–4 of 10 food groups) among:
- 57.3% of rural and urban purchasers and 65.9% of slum purchasers
- 63.5% and 70.2% of adolescents in the same two settings

- 78.3% of adolescents in rural and urban areas and 85.6% in slums
- 60.8–76.2% of adults

#### Knowledge is only weakly linked to what people eat
Despite the steep social gradient in knowledge, the bivariate links between knowledge and diet were weak (Fig. 4b). High nutrition knowledge was found among:
- 35.9% of purchasers with adequate dietary diversity vs 30.8% with inadequate diversity
- 39.4% vs 31.6% among adolescents

#### The household food-safety "last mile"
Most cooks endorsed general hygiene principles:
- 96.1% said hands should be cleaned before cooking.
- 81.7% knew that raw and cooked food should be stored separately.

Specific knowledge was much weaker (Fig. 5a):
- 57.7% did not know how many times cooked food can safely be reheated.
- 52.2% did not know that food should not be stored near chemicals or cleaning supplies.
- 74.5% did not describe washing vegetables whole before cutting them.

Food-safety knowledge was not associated with dietary diversity (P = 0.55).

Self-reported handwashing with soap was close to universal at two critical times: 96.5% before eating and 97.6% after defecation. It was much lower at the critical times that matter most for food:
- 49.7% before cooking
- 42.0% before feeding a child
- 50.5% after cleaning a child's faeces

In slums, only 35.3% reported washing hands with soap before cooking.

`[NEW: observed handwashing station with soap and water present — x% — compared with self-report (Fig. 5b).]`

Between 7.1% and 54.3% of current handwashers attributed the practice to COVID-19 messaging, depending on the critical time. The highest share was for washing after returning home, a COVID-specific behaviour. Almost all (93–100%) said they intended to continue (Fig. 5c). The share attributed to messaging was smallest for food-related critical times such as before cooking (18.7%), which suggests that pandemic campaigns did little to change food-handling hygiene.

#### Knowledge, nutritional status and self-rated health
Low nutrition knowledge was more common among underweight adults. Among purchasers, 52.5% of underweight adults had low knowledge, compared with 33.7% of those of normal weight. It was also more common among people rating their health as bad (65.2%) than very good (29.1%).

### Discussion (~1,000 words)

This is, to our knowledge, the first nationally representative assessment of consumers' nutrition, cooking and food-safety knowledge in Bangladesh. It is also one of few in any LMIC to link such knowledge to measured diets, food insecurity and observed hygiene. Three findings stand out.

**First, knowledge gaps are specific and socially concentrated.** Most people know that a "balanced diet" matters. Far fewer can name iron- or vitamin A-rich foods, know the risks of trans fats or know how to reheat and store food safely. Knowledge rises steeply with education and wealth, and slum adolescents are furthest behind. Targeting by education, wealth and location is therefore justified. The large residual differences between divisions, after adjustment, point to differences in programme intensity and local food cultures that deserve study⁴.

**Second, and most important for policy, knowledge is weakly related to what people actually eat.** It does not protect against the discretionary foods that now reach most adolescents. This is consistent with evidence from high-income countries that nutrition knowledge is necessary but not sufficient for healthy diets¹⁰,¹¹. It extends that evidence to a population in which `[x%]` of households are food-insecure. `[NEW: The attenuation of the knowledge–diet link under food insecurity implies that…]` Where nutrient-dense foods such as eggs, dairy, fruit and vegetables cost far more per calorie than rice¹³, and where packaged snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages are cheap and heavily advertised, knowing what to eat does not ensure that one can eat it.

**Third, the household is a weak link in food safety.** Large shares of cooks are unaware of safe reheating and storage. Handwashing at food-related critical times is far less common than at the times emphasised by pandemic messaging. COVID-19 campaigns appear to have shifted visible, COVID-specific behaviours more than food-handling hygiene.

**Policy implications.** Bangladesh's nutrition and food-safety strategies should keep investing in BCC, but should change what it does:
1. Focus messages on specific, actionable gaps: micronutrient-rich foods, reheating, storage and handwashing before cooking and feeding.
2. Target adolescents, slum residents and people with little schooling, using channels they trust (`[NEW: trusted-source results]`).
3. Above all, pair information with food-environment and affordability measures. Examples are targeted transfers or vouchers for nutrient-dense foods through existing safety nets, and restrictions on marketing discretionary foods to children and adolescents. School curricula and the national community radio could carry food-safety content, as the survey stakeholders recommended.

**Limitations.**
- The survey is cross-sectional, so associations cannot be interpreted causally. Reverse causation (people who eat diverse diets learning more) and residual confounding are possible. We report E-values to gauge robustness.
- Knowledge was measured with closed and semi-open questions. Scores depend on item selection; we provide item-level results and report reliability (`[α]`).
- Diets were assessed by a single 24-hour recall, which captures population-level diversity but not individual usual intake.
- Self-reported hygiene is subject to social-desirability bias, especially during intense COVID-19 messaging. We therefore triangulate with enumerator observation.
- Fieldwork took place during pandemic movement restrictions. Some urban and slum households had to be replaced by neighbouring ones, which may introduce selection bias. We assess this by comparing sample characteristics with MICS 2019 `[NEW]`.
- Almost all cooks were women and most purchasers were men. Sex differences within roles therefore cannot be separated from role effects.

**Conclusion.** In Bangladesh, what households know about food is unequal, and it is only loosely tied to what they eat. National strategies that rely on information alone risk widening rather than closing diet gaps. Making healthy, safe food affordable and accessible must accompany efforts to make it known.

## Methods (~2,500 words; not counted in main-text limit)

### Study design and setting
We conducted a cross-sectional household survey in rural, urban and slum areas of all eight administrative divisions of Bangladesh (Barisal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna, Mymensingh, Rajshahi, Rangpur and Sylhet). It was commissioned by FAO and the Food Planning and Monitoring Unit (FPMU), Ministry of Food, under the Meeting the Undernutrition Challenge (MUCH) project. Data were collected between 1 October and 30 November 2020.

### Sampling
**Rural and urban strata.** The sampling frame was the list of 3,220 enumeration areas (EAs; mean 113 households) of the Bangladesh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2019¹⁸. Each division was stratified into urban and rural domains.

Sample size per domain was calculated with the MICS formula:
- 95% confidence level
- anticipated prevalence 0.5
- design effect 2.5
- margin of error 5%
- target population proportion 0.47
- average household size 4.6
- response rate 90%

This gave 250 households per domain, which we doubled to allow for design effects. We selected 200 primary sampling units (53 urban, 147 rural) by systematic random sampling with independent allocation by stratum. Twenty households per cluster were then drawn by equal-probability systematic selection from a fresh household listing, giving 3,989 households (Table 1 of SI; 11 interviews were lost when an enumerator dropped out).

**Slum stratum.** From the 9,113 slums in the 2014 Census of Slum Areas and Floating Population, we selected 50 slums with probability proportional to size. The slums were stratified into Dhaka plus Mymensingh and the other divisions. We sampled 20 households per slum by systematic random sampling, giving 1,011 households.

**Respondents.** In each household we identified:
1. the person primarily responsible for purchasing food
2. the person primarily responsible for cooking
3. the primary caregiver of a child aged 6–23 months, if present
4. one adolescent aged 10–19, if present

`[State how adolescent selected if >1; overlap of purchaser/cook roles.]`

### Data collection and quality control
Interviews were conducted face-to-face in Bangla by 24 trained field research assistants and 6 supervisors, using a pre-tested structured questionnaire programmed in KoboCollect. Enumerators had 4 days of training. Supervisors observed at least one interview per enumerator per day and back-checked about 5% of households within 48 hours. Data were reviewed daily by a central statistician. Enumerators followed COVID-19 infection-prevention protocols.

### Measures
**Household characteristics.**
- Demography, education and occupation
- Assets, housing materials and land, used to build a wealth index by principal component analysis divided into quintiles¹⁹
- Income, total, food and health expenditure, remittances and safety-net participation
- Internet access
- Livestock

**Food insecurity.** Nine-item HFIAS (4-week recall), categorised as secure, mildly, moderately or severely food-insecure¹⁷, plus six coping strategies.

**WASH.**
- Drinking-water source, treatment and time to collect water; sanitation facility and sharing
- Enumerator-observed handwashing place, with presence of water and soap or other cleansing agent within one minute
- Enumerator-observed toilet cleanliness, kitchen pollution sources and storage of food near chemicals

**Knowledge.** Domain-specific question sets were adapted from FAO KAP guidelines¹⁶, the WHO Five Keys to Safer Food²⁰ and national dietary guidelines:
- *Basic nutrition* (25 items): food groups, healthy diets, nutrient-rich foods, trans fats, diet in pregnancy and lactation
- *Cooking practice* (14 items)
- *Food safety and hygiene* (20 items plus handwashing knowledge)
- *Infant and young child feeding (IYCF)* (11 items; caregivers)

Questions were asked without prompting the answer options. Each correct dichotomous answer scored 1. Multiple-response items scored the fraction of correct options mentioned¹⁶. Domain scores are expressed as a percentage of the maximum. The primary analysis uses the continuous score. Secondary analyses use an absolute threshold (≥60%) and sample tertiles (as in the original report²¹). Internal consistency was `[α]`.

**Diet.**
- 24-hour qualitative recall of 24 food groups, plus 7-day frequency, for each adult and adolescent respondent
- Dietary diversity: 10 food groups; MDD-W ≥5 groups for women 15–49 (ref. 22)
- Discretionary-food score: salty or fried snacks, sweets and sugar-sweetened beverages
- Children 6–23 months: MDD (≥5 of 8 groups), MMF and MAD following WHO/UNICEF guidance

**Anthropometry and health.**
- Height and weight measured twice (a third time if readings differed by more than 0.5 cm or 0.1 kg) and MUAC
- BMI categorised by WHO adult cut-offs; BMI-for-age z-scores (WHO 2007) for adolescents
- Self-rated health on the EQ-VAS (0–100) and the three-item Minimum European Health Module²³

**COVID-19-attributed hygiene change.** For each critical handwashing time, respondents reported whether the practice:
- predated the pandemic
- was adopted because of COVID-19 public health messaging
- would be continued after the pandemic

### Statistical analysis
All analyses accounted for the complex survey design: sampling weights, clustering by primary sampling unit and stratification by division × residence. Prevalences are weighted with 95% confidence intervals.

The models (pre-specified; covariates chosen from a directed acyclic graph, Extended Data Fig. 1) were:
- **Model 1.** Survey-weighted linear regression of knowledge scores on education, wealth quintile, age, sex, residence, division, internet access and media exposure.
- **Model 2.** Survey-weighted Poisson (dietary diversity), logistic (MDD-W) and ordinal (discretionary score) regressions of diet outcomes on knowledge (per 10 percentage points), adjusted for Model 1 covariates, HFIAS category and food expenditure per adult equivalent.
- **Model 3.** Model 2 plus knowledge × HFIAS and knowledge × wealth interactions. Results are shown as average marginal effects.
- **Decomposition.** We decomposed differences in dietary diversity between the richest and poorest quintiles into knowledge, affordability and access components using Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition.

Rare categories were collapsed, and Firth-penalised logistic regression was used where separation occurred. Missing data (`[x%]`) were handled by multiple imputation with chained equations (m = 20), and complete-case results are reported for comparison.

Sensitivity analyses:
- alternative knowledge cut-offs
- exclusion of slum households
- exclusion of respondents who were both purchaser and cook
- leave-one-division-out estimates
- E-values for unmeasured confounding

Tests were two-sided with α = 0.05, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to item-level comparisons. Analyses used `[R 4.x (survey, marginaleffects, logistf, mice) / Stata 18]`.
