We are submitting our Article, **"Nutrition knowledge is socially patterned but weakly linked to diets across Bangladesh"**, for consideration in *Nature Food*.

Governments in low- and middle-income countries invest heavily in nutrition and food-safety information campaigns, on the assumption that better knowledge leads to better diets. We test that assumption at national scale. Our data come from a survey of 5,000 households across all eight divisions of Bangladesh, covering rural, urban and slum strata. We interviewed the four people who shape household diets: food purchasers, cooks, adolescents and caregivers of young children. We combined their knowledge assessments with 24-hour diets, food-insecurity scores, measured anthropometry and enumerator-observed hygiene.

Our main findings are:
- **Knowledge is strongly unequal.** The odds of high nutrition knowledge are about 40 times greater among people with higher-secondary schooling than among those with none. Slum adolescents are furthest behind.
- **Knowledge is weakly linked to diets** `[NEW: effect size]`. It does not protect against discretionary snacks and sugary drinks, which 78–86% of adolescents had eaten in the previous day. `[NEW: The knowledge–diet link largely disappears in food-insecure households.]`
- **Household food safety is a weak link.** Most cooks do not know safe reheating or storage practices. COVID-19 messaging changed pandemic-specific handwashing far more than handwashing before cooking or feeding children.

To our knowledge, this is the first nationally representative assessment of consumer food literacy in Bangladesh, and one of very few in any LMIC to link it to diets, food insecurity and observed practice. The findings matter beyond Bangladesh. They show that information-only strategies are unlikely to improve diets in populations going through the nutrition transition unless healthy, safe food is also made affordable. This conclusion is relevant to readers working on food environments, social protection and food-safety policy.

The study was conducted by Bangladeshi researchers in partnership with the Ministry of Food and FAO, and was approved by the BRAC James P Grant School of Public Health IRB.
