We are submitting our Article, **"Nutrition knowledge does not translate into healthier diets in Bangladeshi households"**, for consideration in *Nature Food*.

Governments in low- and middle-income countries invest heavily in nutrition and food-safety information campaigns, on the assumption that better knowledge leads to better diets. We test that assumption at national scale. Our data are microdata from a nationally representative survey of 5,239 households across all eight divisions of Bangladesh, covering rural, urban and slum strata. We linked the knowledge of cooks, food purchasers and adolescents to their 24-hour diets, household food insecurity (HFIAS), measured anthropometry and hygiene observed by enumerators.

Our main findings are:
- **Knowledge is low and unequal.** Fewer than 14% of respondents answered at least 60% of nutrition questions correctly. Knowledge rose with wealth, household internet access and exposure to nutrition messages, and fell with food insecurity.
- **Knowledge does not translate into better diets.** After accounting for household resources, knowledge was unrelated to dietary diversity: −0.01 food groups per 10-point increase among cooks (95% CI −0.07 to 0.05). It was associated with *higher* odds of eating sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets and fried snacks (odds ratio 1.21; 1.11–1.31). In moderately or severely food-insecure households, higher knowledge accompanied *lower* dietary diversity (P for interaction < 0.001). Knowledge explained essentially none of the rich–poor gap in diet diversity.
- **Hygiene follows resources.** Handwashing places with water and soap were present in 40% of the poorest and 77% of the richest households, independent of food-safety knowledge. COVID-19 messaging changed handwashing before cooking least of all critical times.

To our knowledge, this is the first nationally representative analysis of consumer food literacy in Bangladesh. It is also one of very few in any LMIC to test whether knowledge translates into diets once food insecurity and purchasing power are considered. The findings are relevant beyond Bangladesh. Information-only strategies are unlikely to improve diets during the nutrition transition unless healthy, safe food is also made affordable. This is directly relevant to readers working on food environments, social protection and food-safety policy.
